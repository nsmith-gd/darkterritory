# Maritime landscape research: rules for terrain and railway generation

Research pass for the procedural terrain and railway generator (see `docs/design/linegen-plan.md`). It covers Nova Scotia, New Brunswick and Prince Edward Island, and it is written for turning into parameters, not for reading as a history.

**Method and caveats.** All sourcing came from web search result extracts. The research proxy blocked direct page fetches for every domain tried (Wikipedia, Parks Canada, Canadian Encyclopedia, Dalhousie OJS, canada-rail.com and others). Each figure below comes from the search extract of the URL cited next to it. Figures marked **[est.]** are my own engineering or ecological estimates, drawn from general knowledge of North American practice and not from a source found here. Treat them as starting tuning values to check against maps or DEMs. None of this is spec yet. When a number is adopted it moves into `content/tuning/*.json` with a spec citation, per the project rules.

## 1. Physiography and geology by region

**Atlantic (Southern) Upland: South Shore and Eastern Shore.** This is the largest of five upland fragments. It rises from a "rugged Atlantic coast, marked by many inlets, islands, coves and bays" to **180–210 m** in the interior. It is underlain by **granite, quartzite and slate**, and the granite forms the higher ground ([Canadian Encyclopedia, Geography of NS](https://prod-front.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/geography-of-nova-scotia)). The granite gives "knolls and upland areas with a hummocky, boulder-strewn surface", with "thin and acidic" soils and "large areas of exposed bedrock" ([LAC West Nova theme](https://epe.lac-bac.gc.ca/100/205/301/ic/cdc/west_nova/theme.html)). The Southern Upland around Kejimkujik is "a flat plain lacking the major escarpments, mountains and valleys found in other parts of the province" ([Parks Canada, Kejimkujik geology](https://parks.canada.ca/pn-np/ns/kejimkujik/nature/environnement-environment/geo)). So the relief is low (tens of metres), and the texture is high-frequency knobs, not big hills. NS theme regions separate *Granite Barrens (440)*, *Granite Uplands (451)*, *Shelburne Granite Plain (452)* and *Granite Ridge (453)* ([NS ELC map](https://novascotia.ca/natr/forestry/data/ELC/ELC_MAP_2015.pdf)).

**South Mountain.** Highest point 279.8 m. It rises to 150–210 m along the Annapolis Valley ([Wikipedia, South Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Mountain_(Nova_Scotia))).

**North Mountain (Triassic basalt).** A long, narrow ridge between the valley and the Bay of Fundy. Typical height is 180–240 m, peaking at about 235–260 m ([Wikipedia, North Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Mountain_(Nova_Scotia))).

**Annapolis Valley.** About **155 km** long, from St Marys Bay to Minas Basin ([Wikipedia, Annapolis Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annapolis_Valley)). The floor is "well-drained, stone-free soils on undulating topography", with orchards and dairy farms ([Canadian Encyclopedia, Annapolis Lowlands](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/annapolis-lowlands)). Floor width is about **5–10 km** between the ridge crests **[est. from maps]**.

**Cobequid Hills.** Average height **250–300 m**. The southern edge is the **Cobequid Escarpment, ~200 m high and ~125 km long**, running from Cape Chignecto to Truro along the Cobequid–Chedabucto Fault ([Wikipedia, Cobequid Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobequid_Mountains)). The Wentworth Valley is the lowest pass through them ([Wikipedia, Wentworth Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wentworth_Valley)).

**Cape Breton Highlands.** A plateau averaging **~350 m at its edges and >500 m at its centre**. The maximum is **532 m**, the provincial high point ([Wikipedia, Cape Breton Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Breton_Highlands); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/93541)). It is made of "broad, gently rolling hills, deeply cut by steep-walled river canyons". Rivers follow faults and form "long, steep-sided and V-shaped valleys at the margins" (Grande Anse, Ingonish). The Clyburn, Grande Anse and Chéticamp canyons are fault-guided and glacially widened into U-shapes. On the west coast the plateau "meets the Gulf of St. Lawrence in steep cliffs". The Aspy Fault scarp is "straight, steep" and prone to landslides ([Parks Canada, faults and canyons](https://parks.canada.ca/pn-np/ns/cbreton/decouvrir-discover/environ/geologie-geology/failles-faults)). Above about **400 m** the plateau is taiga: "scrub forest, extensive stretches of boggy wetlands and rocky barrens" ([Parks Canada, Taiga region](https://parks.canada.ca/pn-np/ns/cbreton/decouvrir-discover/environ/eco/taiga)). Canyon depth, plateau to floor, is **200–350 m** **[est.]**.

**Fundy shore.** Joggins cliffs are typically **>30 m**, "alternating grey and reddish-brown", and are quarried by 12 m tides ([UWaterloo, Joggins](https://uwaterloo.ca/wat-on-earth/news/fossil-cliffs-joggins)). The Hopewell Rocks flowerpots are **12–21 m** of red-brown conglomerate and sandstone, undercut at the base by tides ([Wikipedia, Hopewell Rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopewell_Rocks)).

**Northumberland Strait lowlands.** "Elevations rarely exceed 50 m", on fine **red sandstones, siltstones and shales** "conspicuous on the cliffs" ([NS ELC 530 Northumberland Lowlands](https://novascotia.ca/natr/ELA/pdf/500/530NorthumberlandLowlandsProfile.pdf)). Gypsum-bearing Windsor Group rocks underlie the lowlands of Hants and Colchester counties, with **~11,715 ha of karst** and many sinkholes ([NS karst risk maps](https://gov.ns.ca/natr/meb/hazard-assessment/sinkholes.asp)). White gypsum cliffs show "among the green spruce" ([NS Archives, Men in Mines](https://archives.novascotia.ca/meninmines/history/gypsum/)).

**PEI.** Maximum **142 m**, in central Queens County. Nearly level in the west, hilly in the centre, gently rolling in the east. Red sandstone and mudstone, with soil coloured by iron oxide ([Wikipedia, Geography of PEI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Prince_Edward_Island)).

**New Brunswick.** The Caledonia and St. Croix Highlands along the Fundy coast rise **>400 m**. Mount Carleton, in the northwest, is **817 m**. The Saint John River valley is the central lowland corridor, and "valley communities are separated from the north and east shores by uplands and belts of forest" ([Canadian Encyclopedia, Geography of NB](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/geography-of-new-brunswick)).

**Isthmus of Chignecto and Tantramar.** A "low strip of marshy land", **27 km** wide between Fundy and the Northumberland Strait ([Canadian Encyclopedia, Chignecto Bay](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/chignecto-bay)). The Tantramar marshes cover about **25,000 acres (~10,000 ha)**. Acadian dyking from the 1670s had drained ">90 per cent of the Chignecto marshes" by the early 1900s ([Canadian Encyclopedia, Tantramar Marsh](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/article/tantramar-marsh)).

**Generator rules**
- Region height bands (above sea level): Atlantic Upland interior 60–210 m. Granite knob amplitude 3–15 m at 50–300 m wavelength **[est.]**, on a very low-frequency base (>10 km wavelength).
- Cape Breton Highlands: a flat-topped plateau at 350–530 m. Use a terraced or clamped height function, not rolling noise. Cut canyons 200–350 m deep **[est.]** along straight fault lineaments, V-shaped at the margins and U-shaped where glaciated. The plateau edge meets the sea in 150–350 m cliffs **[est.]**.
- Cobequid-type fault ridges: a 250–300 m block with a *linear* 200 m escarpment on one side, 100+ km long. The railway crosses only at the lowest notch, as at Wentworth, around 185 m.
- Annapolis-type valley: two parallel ridges at 180–260 m with a flat floor 5–10 km wide **[est.]** at 10–50 m. One ridge is basalt, with a steep face on the sea side.
- Lowlands (Northumberland, Chignecto, PEI west): 0–50 m, with long, gentle swells of 1–5 km wavelength and 5–20 m amplitude **[est.]**. PEI east and central: rolling, to 142 m.
- Soil and rock colour by region: granite grey-pink and slate dark grey on the Atlantic side; brick-red sandstone and red soil on Fundy, Northumberland and PEI; white gypsum outcrops with sinkhole pits (diameter 5–50 m **[est.]**) in Hants and Colchester.

## 2. Glacial landforms

**Drumlins.** Nova Scotia has about **2,300** ([Port Hawkesbury Reporter](https://porthawkesburyreporter.com/?p=1990)). In the LaHave Drumlins ecodistrict (275,071 ha, New Ross to Kejimkujik) they are "streamlined, teardrop-shaped deposits of glacial till" rising **4–50 m** above the surrounding land ([NS ELC 740 LaHave Drumlins](https://novascotia.ca/natr/ELA/pdf/700/740LaHaveDrumlinsProfile.pdf)). Worldwide they are typically **250–1,000 m long, 120–300 m wide**, with length-to-width ratios of about **2–3:1** ([Wikipedia, Drumlin](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumlin)). Locally they are called "whalebacks", after a pod of breaching whales. Orientation shows ice flow "nearly southerly in southwestern NS becoming southeasterly in eastern Lunenburg and western Halifax counties" ([Canadian Encyclopedia, Drumlin](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/article/drumlin)). A study of 685 Lunenburg drumlins found elongated forms in the centre of the field and higher densities at its margins ([SMU thesis, Schroeder 1993](https://www.smu.ca/geography/vatherine-treena-oeder.html)). Citadel Hill in Halifax is a drumlin about 37 m (120 ft) above sea level, and McNabs Island is made of **nine drumlins** ([Citadel Hill](https://wikipedia2007.classicistranieri.com/c/i/t/Citadel_Hill_aa64.html); [McNabs](https://mcnabsisland.ca/node/16)).

**Eskers and kettles.** Sand and gravel ridges formed 13–15 ka. "Long eskers occur along the northern margin of Kejimkujik and also along West River", and "kettles typically border esker strings" ([NS DNR field trip](https://novascotia.ca/natr/meb/fieldtrip/vista2.asp); [Parks Canada Kejimkujik](https://parks.canada.ca/pn-np/ns/kejimkujik/nature/environnement-environment/geo)).

**Erratics and barrens.** Devonian granite erratics of the South Mountain batholith were "carried for long distances" and left "rugged barrens" at Peggys Cove. Huckleberry and Labrador tea grow on the exposed flats, and pine and birch grow in pockets of deeper soil ([Halifax Trails / The Islands PP brochure](https://parks.novascotia.ca/sites/default/files/the-islands-provincial-park-brochure.pdf); [GSA 2003 abstract](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2003NE/webprogram/Paper50684.html)).

**Lakes.** Lakes and ponds make up **~15 % of the Southern Upland and of Kejimkujik**. They are "shallow, dark brown, acidic", "connected by wandering rivers" ([Parks Canada, Kejimkujik](https://parks.canada.ca/pn-np/ns/kejimkujik/nature/environnement-environment/geo)). Nova Scotia has more than 3,000 named lakes ([Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_lakes_of_Nova_Scotia)).

**Bogs.** Raised or domed bogs rise "several metres above the surrounding terrain", with pools near the crest. The *Atlantic Plateau Bog* type, in Yarmouth and Shelburne counties only, rises "sharply ... to heights of about four metres" and has a flat top with pools. Sunday Lake bog on the Cape Breton plateau covers **>14,000 acres (~5,700 ha)** ([NS Museum natural history; Dal thesis](https://dalspace.library.dal.ca/handle/10222/14131); [Dunraven Bog](https://novascotia.ca/nse/protectedareas/nr_dunravenbog.asp)).

**Generator rules**
- Drumlin fields: ellipsoids 250–1,000 m long, 120–300 m wide, 4–50 m high (mode about 15–30 m). The long axis runs roughly N–S in the southwest, rotating to NW–SE toward Halifax. Make the stoss (up-ice, north) end blunter than the tapered lee end. Density 2–6 per km² in a field **[est.]**, higher at the field edges. Drumlins that reach the coast become eroding headlands and islands (see section 3).
- Eskers: sinuous ridges 5–20 m high, 30–100 m wide at the base, 1–20 km long **[est.]**, with kettle ponds (50–300 m) strung beside them. Eskers are natural railway and road beds.
- Lakes on the upland: 10–20 % water cover. Size is heavy-tailed, from 0.5 to 500 ha. Shapes are irregular, many-armed and island-studded, often elongated along ice flow with an aspect ratio of 2–5 **[est.]**. Lakes are chained by short rivers with rapids at the rock sills.
- Erratics on the granite barrens: 1–5 m boulders at 50–500 per km², with dense boulder fields of 10–30 % surface cover in patches **[est.]**. In till forest, 5–50 per km² visible **[est.]**.
- Exposed bedrock on barrens: 20–60 % of the surface, as whalebacked grey outcrop with glacial striae in the ice-flow direction **[est.]**.
- Bogs: flat to domed patches of 1–500 ha **[est.]**, with the crown 1–4 m above the margin, dark pools near the top, and a fringe of stunted black spruce and tamarack. In the highland taiga, bog patches can reach thousands of hectares.

## 3. Coasts

**Atlantic side.** "Drowned estuaries and headlands producing an indented coast fringed by islands" under continuing sea-level rise, with "drowned forests, eroding headlands, tidal marshes, and beaches driven inland" ([Dalspace thesis](https://dalspace.library.dal.ca/handle/10222/81005)). The Eastern Shore is "dominated by glacial drumlin headlands, sand and gravel beaches and barriers, and shallow backbarrier estuaries and marshes". Drumlin erosion feeds the barriers ([Dalspace 82093](https://dalspace.library.dal.ca/handle/10222/82093)). Gravel barriers roll landward by overwash: the Story Head barrier migrates **~6 m/yr** ([J. Coastal Research](https://journals.flvc.org/jcr/article/view/78493)).

**Fundy.** Mean tidal range at Burntcoat Head is **11.7 m**, the mean spring range is **14.5 m**, and the extreme is about **16 m** ([Wikipedia, Tidal range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_range); [Guinness](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/73813-highest-tide-average)). Minas Basin plus Cobequid Bay is 1,140 km². At extreme low water the intertidal zone is **more than one-third** of the basin, and the flats "extend over 5 km into the basin at low tide" ([Cobequid Bay KBA](https://www.keybiodiversityareas.org/site/factsheet/11227)). The mean range at the Petitcodiac estuary is 10.2 m, and its flats carry 4–13 cm of very soft mud over firmer ground ([Search and Discovery](https://searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2014/90189ace/abstracts/1841475.html)).

**Dykelands.** Nova Scotia has **241 km of dyke and 260 aboiteaux**, with dykes **1–2.5 m high** ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/1.5077253); [NS Agriculture marsh body](https://bac-lac.wayback.archive-it.org/web/20061124050343/http://www.gov.ns.ca/nsaf/rs/marsh/index.shtml)). Dykes were built from cut marsh sods over a central compacted "key" trench, "several feet" high with sloping sides ([Albert County Museum](https://www.albertcountymuseum.com/the-dykes); [dyking spade](https://versicolor.ca/kerr/OttawaHousePresentation_Aug2011/DykingSpade2/DykingSpade2.html)). An aboiteau is a plank sluice with a one-way clapper valve, the box up to 5 ft wide ([Acadia driving tour](https://history.acadiau.ca/tl_files/sites/history/Public%20History/Driving%20Tour%20Booklet%20Final%20Copy.pdf)). At Grand Pré, dyking ran 1682–1755. The 3-mile Wickwire Dyke of 1808 added **8,000 acres** ([Kings County / Saltscapes](https://saltscapes.com/the-verdant-valley/)). On the Tantramar, "the only thing holding back very high tides is the railway line" in places ([Wikipedia, Isthmus of Chignecto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isthmus_of_Chignecto)).

**Fog.** Halifax averages **121 fog days/yr** and Yarmouth **191** ([Climate of Nova Scotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Nova_Scotia)). Fog peaks June–August on the Atlantic coast.

**Generator rules**
- Atlantic coastline: a fractal ria. Coastline dimension about 1.3–1.4 **[est.]**. Long inlets follow drowned valleys and the ice-flow grain (NW–SE), each 1–15 km long and 0.2–2 km wide **[est.]**. The coast is island-strewn, with 5–30 islands per 10 km of coast **[est.]**. Many islands are half-drowned drumlins with an eroding till cliff (5–30 m) on the seaward side and a gravel spit trailing in the lee.
- Headland types: bare granite whaleback slabs sloping into the sea (grey, lichen-banded, at 5–20° slopes), or drumlin till bluffs.
- Beaches: cobble and shingle ridges 1–4 m high **[est.]** tie drumlins together, with salt marsh and a shallow lagoon behind the barrier.
- Fundy coast: red cliffs 10–40 m high with a wave-cut notch at the base. Stacks 12–21 m tall stand 10–100 m offshore on a rock platform. At low water the red-brown mudflats run 0.5–5 km out. Tide range is 10–16 m: model the tide as a 12.42 h sinusoid, with spring/neap variation of about ±25 % **[est.]**.
- Dykelands: straight, sod-faced dykes 1–2.5 m high, base 5–8 m wide, top 1–2 m wide **[est.]**, with a borrow ditch on the marsh side. Behind them lie flat hay fields at a level just below high tide, cut by a rectilinear grid of drainage ditches ("runs") every 20–60 m **[est.]**, with an aboiteau where each creek meets the dyke. Railways run on or beside dyke lines.
- Fog: Atlantic-facing tiles are fog-prone. Use a fog-day probability of 0.33 (Halifax class) to 0.52 (Yarmouth class), 0.6–0.8 in June–August **[est.]**, and lower inland and on the Northumberland side.

## 4. Rivers

The rivers of the Southern Upland (Tusket, Medway, Mersey, LaHave, St. Marys) run "a clear brown colour due to tannic acid" from bog drainage. Granite catchments cannot buffer acid, and many rivers sit below pH 5.4 ([DFO acidification](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/publications/habitat/highlights-faitssaillants/nova-scotia-nouvelle-ecosse/acidification-watersheds-bassins-hydrographiques-eng.html); [Paddle Lunenburg/Queens](https://promisingpractices.cioc.ca/record/LQR0018?Number=51)). Upland drainage is deranged: lake, short rapid over a granite sill, stillwater, lake.

Tidal rivers of the Fundy side: the Petitcodiac, the "chocolate river", meanders **79 km** over a 2,071 km² basin and carries "heavy sedimentation among the heaviest in North America" ([Wikipedia, Petitcodiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petitcodiac_River)). The Shubenacadie still has a rideable tidal bore. The Petitcodiac and Avon bores were degraded by 1960s–70s causeways ([Wikipedia, Bay of Fundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Fundy)).

**Generator rules**
- Upland rivers: 5–40 m wide **[est.]**, alternating 100–500 m stillwaters with 20–100 m boulder rapids at bedrock sills. Falls are 1–5 m **[est.]**. Water is black-brown (albedo near 0.03, tea-coloured in the shallows), with granite boulders breaking the surface. Valleys are shallow (5–30 m) and irregular, not graded.
- Highland rivers: in fault-line canyons, straight reaches 1–10 km long with steep boulder beds.
- Tidal rivers: a channel with steep, glistening red-brown mud banks (bank height equal to the local tide range, 5–12 m at low water) and a mud channel 50–500 m wide **[est.]** that is nearly empty at low tide. The meander wavelength is about 10–14 channel widths (standard fluvial ratio). Bores arrive about 1–2 h after low water at the mouth, 0.3–1 m high **[est.]**.
- Causeways with gates (Avon, Petitcodiac) create silted mud plains downstream and a freshwater headpond upstream.

## 5. Vegetation

**Acadian forest.** The climax types are **hemlock–red spruce–white pine** and **sugar maple–yellow birch–beech** ([Parks Canada Kejimkujik forests](https://parks.canada.ca/pn-np/ns/kejimkujik/nature/flore-flora/forets-forests)). Red spruce is usually dominant, with balsam fir, yellow birch, sugar maple, some red and white pine, and hemlock more common to the southwest ([NS veg type SH5](https://novascotia.ca/natr/forestry/veg-types/sh/sh5.asp); [WWF NA0410](https://www.worldwildlife.org/ecoregions/NA0410)). Mature canopy reaches **~30 m**, and white pine up to **35 m**. Some hemlock and red spruce are more than 400 years old ([NS old-growth report](https://novascotia.ca/natr/forestry/reports/selnsoldgrowthfor.pdf)). Balsam fir is "usually under 40 feet" (~12 m). Trees cover about **80 % of NS** ([Bradt](https://bradtguides.com/autumn-in-nova-scotia)).

**Coastal forest.** White spruce with balsam fir. On exposed headlands, wind and salt spray produce **krummholz** (NS veg type CO2a) ([NS veg type CO2](https://novascotia.ca/natr/forestry/veg-types/co/co2.asp)).

**Highlands.** Even-aged balsam fir (HL1), repeatedly killed by spruce budworm, with windthrow ([NS veg type HL1](https://novascotia.ca/natr/forestry/veg-types/hl/hl1.asp)).

**Barrens.** Veg type OW1: jack pine over huckleberry, black crowberry and reindeer lichen, with rhodora, lambkill and blueberry, on exposed rock ridges and thin till ([NS veg type OW1](https://www.gov.ns.ca/natr/forestry/veg-types/ow/ow1.asp)).

**Old fields.** "Pasture spruce" (OF1: white spruce over aster and goldenrod) is "a unique and familiar part of Nova Scotia's rural landscape". The stands are even-aged, short-lived, and die off to insects. The wetter variant is tamarack with speckled alder ([NS Old Field group](https://novascotia.ca/natr/forestry/veg-types/of/of.asp)). Along the abandoned H&SW line the flora includes speckled alder, wild rose, mountain ash, red maple, birch, tamarack and white pine ([Jordan River Trail](https://novascotia.com/de/listing/jordan-river-trail/)).

**Clearcuts.** Defined as ≥2 ha and ≥20 m wide. Clearcutting was **~90–95 %** of the harvest around 2010–13, about 40,000 ha/yr ([CBC](https://amp.cbc.ca/news/canada/nova-scotia/nova-scotia-forests-still-being-clearcut-at-high-rate-despite-reductions-1.3160873)).

**Blueberry fields.** About 75 % of NS production comes from Cumberland County. The fields are pruned by burning or flail-mowing, half the acreage each year, so each field is on a 2-year cycle ([Dalhousie wild blueberry](https://cdn.dal.ca/content/dam/dalhousie/images/sites/wild-blueberry/pdfs/Growing_Wild_Lowbush_Blueberries_NS.pdf)).

**Fall colour.** Late September to late October, peaking in the second week of October. Red maple goes brilliant red, sugar maple orange-yellow, and tamarack yellow late. Huckleberry and blueberry turn barrens and fields red ([NS Leaf Watch](https://news.novascotia.ca/en/2000/10/26/autumn-leaf-watch-weekly-report)).

**Generator rules**
- Forest mix on zonal upland sites (by stem): red spruce 30–50 %, balsam fir 15–30 %, red maple 10–20 %, and white or yellow birch, hemlock and white pine 5–15 % each **[est.]**. Hardwood hilltops and ridges (sugar maple, yellow birch, beech) cover 10–30 % of upland area, on the crests and upper slopes of drumlins and ridges.
- Heights: mature spruce and fir 15–22 m, supercanopy white pine 25–35 m, balsam fir 8–15 m, black spruce in bogs 2–8 m, coastal krummholz 0.5–3 m, flagged and leaning downwind **[est. except the pine and canopy figures cited above]**. Stems per ha: mature 600–1,500, regenerating 3,000–10,000 **[est.]**.
- Coast gradient: from open rock and heath, through krummholz, to stunted white spruce at 3–8 m, to full forest, over 20–300 m from the shore depending on exposure **[est.]**.
- Barrens (granite ridges, coastal heads): ground cover 30–60 cm high of huckleberry, blueberry, crowberry and sheep laurel, with pale grey-green reindeer lichen mats and scattered jack pine or black spruce 2–6 m. They turn deep red in October.
- Bogs: tamarack (gold in late October, bare in winter) and black spruce spires over a sphagnum and heath mat.
- Rail and road rights of way: speckled alder thickets 2–6 m **[est.]** on wet margins, then birch, poplar and pin cherry. The mown or sprayed zone is 3–8 m from the track centre **[est.]**. Abandoned grades close to alder in 10–20 years **[est.]**.
- Old fields: rectangular patches of even-height white spruce (5–15 m, dense), next to or containing ruined foundations, fence lines and rock piles.
- Clearcut patches: 2–100 ha, polygon-edged, with slash, scattered stubs of standing dead trees, and a 20 m minimum width. Cover 5–15 % of forest land per decade **[est.]**.
- Blueberry fields (Cumberland): large, bare-looking, low red and brown fields on sandy uplands, with a checkerboard of burned and unburned halves.

## 6. Human landscape

- **Houses**: Early Lunenburg buildings were one-storey Cape Cods (steep roof, loft, central chimney). The "Lunenburg bump" is a five-sided dormer over a porch or bay, on **≥20 %** of buildings in the old town ([Skyrise Cities](https://skyrisecities.com/news/2016/10/lunenburg-dormer-trademark-small-town-nova-scotia); [Patriquin Architects](https://patriquinarchitects.com/traveling-architect-lunenburg-nova-scotia/)).
- **Fishing villages**: silver-grey shingled fish shacks on stilts over the water, and crib wharves ([Peggys Cove](https://www.worldatlas.com/cities/2026-s-11-most-scenic-the-maritimes-towns.html); [Historic Acadian Village, Pubnico](https://levillage.novascotia.ca/whats-on/tour-historic-acadian-village-nova-scotia)).
- **Churches**: white wood-frame Gothic Revival with lancet windows and a central tower. They often stand on a hill with the graveyard beside or behind, fenced with page wire ([Cape Negro Church](https://heritageguide.ca/cape-negro-church-cape-negro-nova-scotia); [Brenton Methodist](https://heritageguide.ca/brenton-methodist-church-brenton-nova-scotia)).
- **Lighthouses**: the square, tapered wooden tower was standard from the 1840s into the 21st century ([Pictou Island South, Parks Canada DFHD](https://www.pc.gc.ca/apps/dfhd/page_hl_eng.aspx?id=14894)).
- **Coal**: company houses built 1850–1920 in Sydney, Glace Bay, Dominion, New Waterford, Sydney Mines and Inverness, in districts named after pits ("Number 2", "The Hub") ([National Trust Canada](https://nationaltrustcanada.ca/?p=13167)). The Pictou coalfield covers about 18 × 6 km and has 15 seams. The Foord seam, 13.5 m thick, burns and explodes ([Museum of Industry](https://museumofindustry.novascotia.ca/node/89)). Springhill is the third field ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springhill_Coal_Mining_National_Historic_Site)).
- **Annapolis Valley**: more than 1,000 farms and orchards. The Windsor and Annapolis Railway (1869) made the apple trade ([Kings County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_County,_Nova_Scotia)).
- **Gypsum quarries**: at Windsor, Falmouth, Hantsport, Miller Creek, Wentworth, Walton and McKay Settlement ([NS Archives](https://archives.novascotia.ca/meninmines/history/gypsum/)).

**Generator rules**
- Villages are linear along the shore road or rail, not nucleated. Houses sit 30–150 m apart with gable-end or eave-to-road orientation mixed **[est.]**. Palette: white, grey shingle, barn red, ochre, dark green trim. Few lit windows in the dark setting.
- Fishing coves: 3–20 sheds on piles or cribs at the waterline, 1–3 wharves 20–100 m long, lobster-trap stacks 1–2 m high on the wharves **[est.]**, and a square tapered white lighthouse on the outermost point.
- A church every 3–10 km along settled routes **[est.]**, on the highest local ground, with its cemetery beside it.
- Coal towns: dense grids of identical 1.5- or 2-storey double company houses, a steel headframe (20–40 m **[est.]**), a dark conical spoil pile, sidings, and a wharf with a coal pier. Clusters of pits are 1–5 km apart.
- Farmland: long-lot fields perpendicular to rivers (Acadian and Planter), dykeland hay grids, orchard rows 5–8 m apart **[est.]**, and hedgerows and rock piles at field edges. Inland, many fields are abandoned and growing up in spruce.
- Industry: gypsum quarries as white terraced pits next to the rail, sawmills with sawdust piles and log booms on the rivers.

## 7. Railways

**Intercolonial (ICR, 1872–76).** Sandford Fleming surveyed it and was Chief Engineer. The route follows the North Shore and Chaleur Bay, away from the US border ([Canadian Encyclopedia, ICR](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/intercolonial-railway)). The line (~1,100 km) was built "to keep the rail lines straight and level". Nearly all bridges were **iron, not wood**, and the roadbed was **raised to shed snow** ([Canada.ca ICR](https://www.canada.ca/en/news/archive/2015/07/intercolonial-railway.html); [CSCE ICR](https://legacy.csce.ca/en/?p=200647)). Fleming called Moncton–Truro the section with "the most crooked alignment, the greatest extent of curvature, the sharpest curves, the highest bridge, the deepest embankment, the steepest grade". It climbs from the Wallace valley to **Folly Lake summit, 607 ft (185 m)**, and has **"eleven miles of steep grades varying from 0.80 in 100 to 1 in 100"** ([Halifax City Minutes 1908, quoting Fleming](https://legacycontent.halifax.ca/archives/HalifaxCityMinutes/documents/102-1a-1908-04-30to1908-04-30p0380-0400.pdf)). The Miramichi crossings were two iron bridges on stone piers, 12 spans in all, including a 180 ft truss ([Wikipedia, Hillsborough River Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillsborough_River_Bridge)). There was a snow shed near Wentworth in 1875. The 1905 storm left drifts **up to 5 m**, and one blockade of 27 miles needed 1,000 shovellers ([Wikipedia ICR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercolonial_Railway); [AADL](https://aadl.org/node/422903)). The standard station was one storey with a dominant hipped roof and broad bracketed eaves. The 1867 "way stations" combined passenger and freight, with long platforms ([Canadian Encyclopedia, railway station](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/railway-station)). The line crosses the Tantramar between Sackville and Amherst on the marsh edge, and in places the grade is now the de facto sea dyke ([Wikipedia, Isthmus of Chignecto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isthmus_of_Chignecto)).

**ICR Cape Breton (1890–91).** Point Tupper to Sydney. It "crosses the North Bras d'Or Uplands before skirting the shores of Bras d'Or Lake along the Boisdale Hills" ([Wikipedia, CBCNS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Breton_and_Central_Nova_Scotia_Railway)). The **Grand Narrows Bridge** (1890) is **516 m**, the longest railway bridge in NS: seven riveted steel trusses of 73.76 m on cut-stone piers, with a swing span ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Narrows_Bridge)). Orangedale (1886) is a two-storey Victorian wooden station ([NS Railway Heritage](https://novascotiarailwayheritage.com/orangedale/station-history/)). The **Canso Causeway** (1955) is a **1,385 m** rock-fill causeway carrying road and single track, with a 24 m × 570 m canal lock ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canso_Causeway)).

**Dominion Atlantic (DAR).** 288 mi (463 km), headquartered in Kentville, Windsor Junction to Yarmouth through the Valley, with the Midland branch Windsor–Truro opened in 1901 ([Wikipedia DAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion_Atlantic_Railway)). The Avon "Basket Bridge" at Windsor carried trains 1870–1915 ([Wikipedia, Avon River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avon_River_(Nova_Scotia))). The Midland's "massive" Shubenacadie bridge at South Maitland spanned the tidal-bore river, and only an abutment survives ([Wikipedia South Maitland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Maitland,_Nova_Scotia)). Station mileposts from Kentville in 1949: Port Williams 5.23, Wolfville 7.02, Grand Pré 9.92, Horton Landing 11.23, Avonport 13.12, Hantsport 18.06, Windsor 25.06 ([Wikipedia DAR timetable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion_Atlantic_Railway)). That is a mean gap of **3.6 mi (5.8 km)**, ranging 1.3–7.0 mi. The *Evangeline* had **23 flag stops** in 1956 ([Wikipedia, Evangeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangeline_(train))).

**Halifax & South Western (1901–18).** Mackenzie and Mann built it cheaply, "wandering" through Chester, Mahone Bay, Lunenburg, Bridgewater, Liverpool, Shelburne and Barrington to Yarmouth. It was so curved that it became "**Hellish Slow & Wobbly**", and Halifax to Yarmouth took 11 hours ([Wikipedia H&SW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halifax_and_South_Western_Railway); [traingeek](https://blog.traingeek.ca/2018/05/the-halifax-and-southwestern-railway.html)). A Wikipedia extract gives total curvature as 1,025°, scope unverified. The Martins River trestle and bridge crosses a **77 m** gap ([CMEA](https://cmea-agmc.ca/en/node/541)).

**PEI Railway (1871–).** 3 ft 6 in gauge until 1930, 280 mi in all. The contract paid per mile with no route fixed, so builders went "around instead of over obstacles like hills and streams". The main line was 147 mi on a 120 mi island, and "over a third of the line was curves". The maximum grade was **66 ft/mi (1.25 %)**, and **74 ft/mi (1.4 %)** near Souris ([Kensington CHIP](https://kensington.ca/wp-content/themes/ktown/chip/english/railway/beginning.htm); [Wikipedia PEIR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Edward_Island_Railway)).

**Inverness & Richmond (1901).** Port Hastings to Inverness along the west coast of Cape Breton, hauling coal to a pier ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverness_and_Richmond_Railway)). **Sydney & Louisburg (1910–68)**: a 63 km main line and 187 km of total track, coal-only in character, through Glace Bay and Morien ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_and_Louisburg_Railway)).

**Rock cuts.** The Halifax South End cut (1912–18) removed 2.5 million yd³, mostly slate and quartzite, **up to 20 m deep**, in blasts up to 30 ft deep ([CSCE Ocean Terminals](https://legacy.csce.ca/en/historic-site/ocean-terminals/)).

**Maintenance.** CN sections were about **10 miles (16 km)**, each with a foreman and section house ([Island Lives](https://islandlives.ca/sites/default/files/2023-09/ilives209220-p_023.txt)). In the steam era, water tanks usually stood at stations, with gaps above ~40–50 mi treated as too long ([PADWRR Kenwell tank](https://padwrr.com/canadian-northern-railway/canadian-northern-railway-long-lake-subdivision/kenwell-water-tank/)).

**Generator rules**
- Route classes, each a tuning profile:
  - **Mainline (ICR)**: ruling grade 0.6–1.0 %, curve radius ≥ 350–600 m (about 3–5° curves) **[est.]**. Long tangents. Big iron or steel trusses on stone piers, high embankments, deep cuts. Summits at gaps (185 m class).
  - **Coast and colonization line (H&SW, I&R)**: ruling grade 1.0–1.5 %, radius 175–350 m (5–10°) **[est.]**. It hugs the coast and lake shores, heads each cove instead of bridging it, and bridges only at a narrows. Short timber trestles, 20–80 m.
  - **Contour line (PEIR)**: grade ≤ 1.25–1.4 %. More than a third of its length is curved. Route length is about 1.2–1.3 × the straight-line distance (147/120 = 1.23). Almost no cuts or fills: the cost function should heavily penalise earthwork and hardly penalise length.
  - **Coal branch (S&L)**: short, 1–2 % grades, dense sidings and piers.
- Valleys and coasts: route along valley floors and on the dry terrace just above the dykes or marsh. Cross marsh on low embankments of 1–3 m, and let a straight tangent follow the dyke line. Cross tidal rivers at the narrowest point with long bridges: 100–500 m of trusses or trestle on piers, the rail 10–20 m above low water to clear the tide range plus a margin. Use swing spans on navigable water.
- Granite country: frequent short rock cuts 3–10 m deep (up to 20 m in exceptional cases), 50–300 m long, with vertical drill-scarred walls. Low fills between knobs. Culverts at each lake chain.
- Causeways: rock fill 200–1,400 m across bays and straits (Canso class), usually modern. Early lines used trestle.
- Snow: raised grade of 0.5–1.5 m above local ground on mainlines **[est.]**, snow fences 20–50 m upwind in open marsh and barrens **[est.]**. Rare snow sheds at cuts in the gaps. Drifts up to 5 m.
- Stations: 3–10 km apart in settled country (mean about 6 km), 10–20 km in empty upland. Flag stops (a shelter or nothing, plus a signal post) fill the gaps. Station styles: one-storey hipped roof with bracketed eaves (standard ICR), or two-storey wood Victorian at junctions. A freight shed and long low platform at each.
- Section houses every ~16 km (10 mi). Water tanks every 15–40 km **[est.]**, placed at stations or at streams and lakes. Coal towers at division points of 100–200 km **[est.]**.
- Bridge counts: a Southern Upland line crosses water every 1–3 km (lake necks, brooks) **[est.]**. On a Fundy-side line, expect a tidal-river bridge every 10–30 km **[est.]**.

## 8. Weather and light

The fog frequencies are in section 3. Nor'easters bring "heavy snow, ice, rain, and strong easterly winds". Snow often turns to ice pellets and freezing rain overnight. A single January event brought 15 mm of freezing rain with gusts over 100 km/h ([ECCC top weather 2014](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/top-ten-weather-stories/top-ten-weather-stories-archive-2014/atlantic-highlights.html); [CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/1.7415053)). November in Halifax has about 13 rain days and 129 mm of rain, and only about 2 clear days ([avionero climate summary](https://avionero.com/New-York_NYC-Halifax_YHZ/weather/november)). Annual precipitation in the LaHave area is 1,400–1,500 mm ([NS ELC 740](https://novascotia.ca/natr/ELA/pdf/700/740LaHaveDrumlinsProfile.pdf)).

**Generator rules**
- Late-autumn default: overcast 80–90 % of the time, drizzle or rain on about 45 % of days, 2–4 clear days a month.
- Nor'easter event: a 12–36 h sequence of east wind at 50–100+ km/h, then snow, then ice pellets and freezing rain, then rain. Glaze on rails, wires and trees. The wind-side coast gets spray and fog.
- Fog: advection fog rolls in off the Atlantic and fills the coves first. Visibility falls to 50–200 m, and fog lifts at inland elevation. Fundy fog sits in the bay and around the flats.
- Dusk comes early: sunset around 16:35 local in late November at 45°N **[est.]**, with a long grey twilight.

## Top 15 rules that make it feel like the Maritimes (ranked by visual impact from a train window at night or dusk)

1. **Dark spruce wall close to the track.** Narrow-crowned red spruce and fir, 15–22 m, spiky against a grey sky, 5–15 m from the rail behind an alder fringe. This is the default view.
2. **Black lakes that appear suddenly.** On the Atlantic Upland the line skirts or crosses a lake every 1–3 km. The water is black and flat, with granite boulders, a lake-neck trestle, and a far shore of spruce.
3. **Granite everywhere.** Short grey rock cuts with drill scars, whaleback outcrops, and car-sized erratics scattered in the woods and barrens.
4. **Red mud and a tide that is out.** On the Fundy side, glistening red-brown mudbanks 5–12 m deep either side of a nearly empty channel, crossed on a long truss. The height of the mud tells you the state of the tide.
5. **Fog.** Coast tiles are foggy a third to half of the nights. Fog fills the coves first, and headlights and lamps turn to halos.
6. **Ria coast with islands.** The line curves around the head of each cove. Views open onto island-strewn water with drumlin islands and a square white lighthouse flashing on the outer point.
7. **Dykeland flats.** Absolutely flat marsh hay fields under a big sky, with a dyke line, ditches and wooden sluices. The railway runs on the dyke, and the sea sits higher than the fields on the other side.
8. **Heath barrens.** Open, knee-high red and brown heath with pale lichen, stunted jack pine and black spruce, erratic boulders, and a wide empty horizon. Deep red in October.
9. **Constant wandering curvature.** The south shore and PEI profiles curve most of the time. The lamp beam sweeps across trees, and the train rarely sees far ahead.
10. **White church and graveyard on the rise.** A white wooden steeple on the highest ground of every settlement, with its headstones beside it. The best landmark for dusk silhouettes.
11. **Fishing coves on stilts.** Grey shingle sheds on piles, crib wharves, stacked traps, and lobster boats sitting on the mud at low tide.
12. **Old-field spruce and ruined farms.** Even-height pockets of dense white spruce on former fields, with foundation holes, rock piles, leaning fence posts and dark farmhouses.
13. **Coal country.** Steel headframes, black spoil cones, rows of identical company houses, and sidings to a coal pier. This is the industrial dark heart, belonging to Cape Breton, Pictou and Springhill.
14. **Plateau and gorge in the highlands.** Flat taiga with bogs at 350–530 m, then the line drops through a fault-straight canyon to the coast or crosses it on a high iron bridge.
15. **Late-autumn palette and weather.** Grey-green conifers, red maple and huckleberry, gold tamarack in the bogs, a low overcast sky, and nor'easter glaze on the rails and wires.
